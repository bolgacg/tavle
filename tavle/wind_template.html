A work sample · one trading idea tested on public data, and the public forecast it would rely on audited · this page builds no forecast

Does the wind forecast already know tomorrow's price?

This page does two things and builds no forecast. It takes one trading idea and tests whether it survives the data, the way a desk would have to test it before risking money: on public data, with the questions, the split and the costs fixed before any result was seen. And it audits the forecast that idea would rely on. The idea: Denmark sets its electricity price in an auction held the day before delivery; the single biggest thing that moves that price is how much wind will blow; Energinet, the grid operator, publishes wind forecasts; so predict tomorrow's auction price from the forecast and trade on it. Three results. The idea cannot be tested as stated, because the forecast is published six hours after the auction (act one). Wind explains of the hour-to-hour price, and a plain model that uses it is about as accurate as simply quoting yesterday's price (act two). The forecast has under-predicted DK1 wind by a growing margin since 2023, , and the cost of its errors has a direction on average but not in the typical hour (act three).

Before the study · four things to know
DK1, DK2 Denmark's two price zones: DK1 is Jutland and Funen in the west, where most of the wind turbines stand; DK2 is Zealand and Copenhagen in the east. Each zone gets its own hourly price.
day-ahead auction the market where tomorrow's electricity is sold. It closes at noon and sets one price for every hour of the next day, in euros per megawatt-hour; a megawatt-hour is roughly what three hundred homes use in an hour.
wind forecast Energinet's estimate of how many megawatt-hours of wind power each zone will produce in a given hour. It is issued several times: the evening before, the same morning, five hours ahead, one hour ahead.
imbalance price the price a producer pays or receives afterwards for every megawatt-hour it delivered short or over what it sold in the auction. It is how a wrong forecast turns into money.
Act one · The order of events

Can the forecast predict the auction at all? Only if it exists before the auction closes

Whether a forecast can predict a price depends on one thing before any statistics: whether the forecast is published before or after the price is set. If it comes after, a model that uses it to "predict" the price is reading the answer off the back of the page, and it will look excellent in a backtest. The idea cannot be tested as stated. The auction closes at 12:00 the day before delivery. Energinet's day-ahead wind forecast is published at 18:00 the same day, six hours later, according to its own documentation quoted above. Any model that predicts the day-ahead price from that forecast on this dataset is using information that did not exist when the price was set. The study records the pre-registered strategy as untestable rather than swapping in a different forecast and reporting a result, because a backtest built on the later forecast would look good and mean nothing.
What can still be tested. Every forecast horizon is published before the delivery hour, and the imbalance price, the cost of being wrong, is settled after it. So three questions remain that the data can answer: how much of the price is wind at all (act two), how large and how biased the forecast errors are, and whether those errors push the imbalance price in a predictable direction (act three).
Act two · How much of the price is wind, and how good is the model

Wind explains a quarter to a third of the hour-to-hour price; a plain model using it is about as good as yesterday's price

Act one settled that the forecast cannot be used to trade the auction. It can still answer a different question: how much of the price movement is wind. That matters for act three, because if wind drives the price, then a wind forecast error is a price error, and price errors cost money. This act fits a plain model of the price and measures how much expected wind adds to it, and then asks the question any reader should ask of a model: how wrong is it, in euros, compared with doing nothing clever.

what was trainedA least-squares model of the hourly day-ahead price with hour-of-day and month effects, fitted twice: without wind, and with Energinet's expected wind as a share of consumption. Fitted on the odd weeks of each year and scored on the even weeks of the same year, so the level of prices, which changed tenfold between 2020 and 2022, cannot flatter the result. Years from 2024 are the holdout: data kept unread until the code was frozen, then scored once.

how to readOne pair of bars per year. The grey bar is how much of the hour-to-hour price variation the plain model explains (R squared, 0 to 1); the coloured bar is the same model once expected wind is added. The difference is wind's share. Years marked * are the holdout.

Wind is a large driver of the price in every year and both zones. Adding expected wind to the plain model raises R squared by across the years scored, with the largest gains in calm years and the smallest in 2026, when prices moved for other reasons. Because the forecast is published after the auction, this measures how much of the price is wind, not whether a desk could have known it in time.

how to readLower is better. "With wind" and "without wind" are the two fitted models; "yesterday" is the same hour one day earlier; "mean" is the average of the training weeks. The typical price and its spread give the scale.

The model is an explanation, not a forecasting tool, and the numbers say so. With wind it is more accurate than the plain model in every year, and it beats yesterday's price in of the zone-years, by a few euros. It loses to yesterday's price in the volatile years and in 2026. A trading desk's model would use the forecast issued before the auction, gas prices, interconnector flows and yesterday's price together; this one is deliberately the simplest thing that answers act two's question.
The DK1 to DK2 price gap is not explained by the difference in expected wind. The gap correlates with the wind-share difference at in training and on the holdout, which is no relationship with a sign that flips. The gap is about whether the cable between the zones is full, and that is a different study. What act two hands to act three: wind moves the price, so the forecast's errors are worth pricing.
Act three · How wrong the forecast is, and what that costs

The forecast misses by a few hundred megawatt-hours, it has under-predicted DK1 wind since 2023, and its errors cost money on average but not in the typical hour

A producer that delivers less than it sold pays the imbalance price for the shortfall; one that delivers more receives it. So a forecast error is a cash flow. This act audits Energinet's forecast; it does not replace it. It measures the error at each horizon against the two guesses anyone could make without a forecast, shows how the day-ahead error has drifted, and asks whether the direction of the error tells you the direction of the cost.

The drift: is the day-ahead forecast biased, and which way

Bias is the average of actual wind minus forecast wind, by month. Zero means the forecast is right on average; positive means more wind arrived than was forecast.

how to readOne point per month. The dashed vertical line marks January 2024, where the holdout begins: the study's code was frozen before any data right of the line was looked at, so what happens there could not have been tuned for. what moves it

Does the direction of the error predict the direction of the cost?

Every hour is put into one of ten bins by how wrong the evening-before forecast was, from "far less wind than forecast" (bin 1) to "far more" (bin 10). For each bin, the average gap between the imbalance price and the auction price. If being wrong had a predictable cost, the bars would run from one sign to the other.

how to readColoured bars are the mean gap in euros per megawatt-hour in that bin; grey bars are the median. A positive gap means the imbalance price was above the auction price, which costs a producer that delivered short. take from it

On average the sign is right; hour by hour it is not. When more wind arrives than the evening forecast said, the imbalance price sits below the auction price on average, and above it when less arrives, in both zones and in both periods. But the sign of the gap agrees with the sign of the error in only of training hours and of holdout hours, because the gap is exactly zero in of hours and small in most of the rest. The averages are made by a minority of hours when the system is badly out of balance.
A rule added after the finding, scored once on the holdout

Because the pre-registered strategy could not be tested, one rule was added afterwards and labelled as added: when the same-morning forecast revises the evening forecast by a large amount, take a position on the sign of the imbalance gap, with the costs fixed in the pre-registration. It stands in for an intraday position; intraday prices are not in the public data.

how to read"Right sign" is how often the position had the right direction; "median hour" is what the middle hour earned after costs; "best decile as share of total" above 1 means the best tenth of hours earned more than everything combined, so the rest lost money together.

This rule is not a strategy, and the numbers are why. By the pre-registered metric it is positive on the holdout in both zones. It is also wrong in most hours, its median hour loses exactly its costs, and its best tenth of hours earns more than the whole result. What that says is that the information sits in the large revisions on the few days the system is badly out of balance; whether that can be captured depends on intraday prices this page does not have. Reporting that is more useful to a desk than a smooth equity curve would have been.
Method, limits, terms

How this was done, and its limits

Two more Energinet datasets added to the tavle platform: the forecasts at every horizon since November 2019, and the hourly imbalance settlement to March 2025. Fifty-seven tests pass on the models. The questions, metrics, holdout and cost model were written down before any result was computed; the changes made after reading Energinet's documentation are listed in the same file with the reason. Training is November 2019 to December 2023; the holdout from January 2024 was read exactly once. The model card's baselines were added on 30 August 2026, after the study, and are labelled as such in the repository.

Limits

Day-ahead auction results and hourly imbalance prices only: no intraday prices, so the added rule is a proxy and says so. Wind is offshore plus onshore as Energinet reports them, and the settlement includes small plants the forecast may not; that is one candidate for the drift, alongside new capacity, and the page tests the capacity candidate only as far as Energinet's installed-capacity table allows. The R squared model is deliberately plain. Two zones, one country, and a study written by one person who chose the questions.

Five terms

day-ahead price the price fixed in the auction held the day before, one per hour per zone
forecast horizon how far before the hour a forecast was made: the evening before, the same morning, five hours, one hour
imbalance price what a producer pays or receives per megawatt-hour for delivering less or more than it sold
look-ahead using information in a backtest that was not available at the time of the decision
holdout the part of the data kept unread until the method was frozen, so a result cannot be tuned to it
