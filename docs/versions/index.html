One hour of wind has six numbers. Which one should a desk act on?

Energinet publishes the wind for one hour six times: four forecasts, a real-time feed, and a settlement that arrives nine to fifteen days later and is revised for two years. A desk sees them in that order and acts on whichever is on its screen. This page puts the six on one grid, measures how far each is from the last, fits a rule for the pipeline to watch the one that matters most, and prices acting on the wrong one.

Before the study · five things to know

Denmark has two price zones. DK1 is Jutland and Funen, with most of the turbines; DK2 is Zealand and Copenhagen. Every hour has one wind number per zone, in megawatt-hours; a megawatt-hour runs about three hundred homes for an hour.

Energinet, the grid operator, forecasts each hour's wind four times: at 18:00 the evening before, at 06:00 the same morning, five hours ahead, and one hour ahead. A producer sells its own forecast into the day-ahead auction, which closes at noon the day before; Energinet's 18:00 number is the first public one, and the later ones are what an intraday desk trades on.

During the hour a real-time feed reports wind every five minutes, scaled up from the control room's measurements. Energinet's note on that dataset: "Errors will occur, and will generally not be corrected." This page averages the twelve readings to one hourly number.

Nine to fifteen days later the metered settlement arrives: what was actually produced, the number everyone is invoiced on. Energinet: 99 percent correct after 15 days, 99.9 percent after three months, revised for up to two years.

A producer that sold one number and delivered another pays the imbalance price on the difference. That is how a wrong version becomes an invoice; act four uses it. Every number on this page is generated by the build script from the data, not typed.

The bias watch: the feed against the settlement, month by month

The tolerance catches a broken hour. It cannot catch a feed that reads five percent high for a month, because five percent is inside it. So the pipeline also sums the last thirty settled days, feed minus settlement over settlement, and warns beyond a band of percent. The band is a design choice, not a fit; the method section says what it would have done in 2025.

how to readOne point per month: the feed's total minus the settlement's total, as a percent of the settlement. The shaded band is the warning band. what moves it

Rebuild yesterday: what changed under the desk overnight

Every night the platform re-fetches the last three days of every source and the last hundred days of the settlement, rebuilds every table, and the newest fetch wins. That is the right rule, and it is also how a number changes without anyone noticing. So before it forgets last night's rows, the pipeline compares them with tonight's, version by version, and writes the differences to a log that is committed with the page.

Act four · What acting on a version costs

The invoice is a forecast problem; the feed's drift is a reconciliation problem

why it mattersThe versions differ by hundreds of megawatt-hours. Whether that matters depends on the price of the difference, and on which part of the difference a desk can do anything about.

the producerOwns 5 percent of the zone's wind, sells one version, delivers the settled amount, and pays the imbalance price on the difference: the economics of the wind study on this site, reused unchanged. period1 November 2021, when single pricing began, to 4 March 2025, when hourly imbalance settlement ended. the splitEach version's error is the forecast part (version minus feed) plus the measurement part (feed minus settlement). Method, model card, limits, terms

How this was done, and what it cannot say

dataEnerginet's Forecasts_Hour (four horizons, wind only, since November 2019), ElectricityProdex5MinRealtime (five-minute feed, landed from January 2020, five columns of fifteen requested), ProductionConsumptionSettlement (metered, re-fetched a hundred days back every night), RegulatingBalancePowerdata (hourly imbalance prices to March 2025) and the day-ahead prices carried by the tavle platform. transformationsA staging table per source, typed and deduplicated by newest fetch. One mart puts the six versions on the union of every hour any of them exists for, so a missing version is a null a test can see, not a row lost in a join. A second mart unpivots it to one row per version with a version column, which is what a board and a test want. testsFour new ones with a severity each: the fitted tolerance (fails the build), the feed's completeness over its last thirty days (fails), the settlement's lag against Energinet's promised fifteen days (warns), and the thirty-day bias band (warns). A late or drifting source must not take the morning board down; a broken one must. splitThe tolerance was fitted on 2025 and checked on 2026. The shape of the rule, a percentile with a megawatt-hour floor, was chosen after looking at the 2025 distribution; nothing about 2026 was looked at before the check was run. The 5 percent bias band is a design choice: in 2025 it would have warned in . oversightA nightly task diffs the recent window of the version mart against the previous night's, appends to a committed log, and the page is rebuilt from it. The log began on ; Energinet's claim that the settlement is 99 percent correct after 15 days will be checked here as nights accumulate, and is quoted, not tested, until then.
Model card: the tolerance rule

What it is: a pass/fail rule on the real-time hour against the settled hour. What it predicts: whether an hour of the feed is usable as a stand-in for the settlement. Features: one number, the disagreement, with a percentage threshold and a megawatt-hour floor. Fitted on 2025, checked on 2026. Error in the reader's units: the share of hours flagged, next to what the 5 percent rule anyone would write does.

how to readPer zone and year: hours with all six versions, the share the fitted rule flags, the share the 5 percent rule flags. The rule is good at its job, which is to be quiet unless an hour is broken; it is no use for drift, which is why the bias watch exists.

Limits

Six terms

version one of the six numbers published for the same hour of wind in the same zone
day-ahead forecast Energinet's forecast published at 18:00 the evening before, the first of the six
real-time feed the five-minute wind measurement, scaled up and never corrected, averaged here to the hour
settlement the metered production published nine to fifteen days later and revised for two years, the last of the six
tolerance how far the feed may sit from the settlement before the pipeline flags the hour
imbalance price the price a producer pays or receives on the difference between what it sold and what it delivered
