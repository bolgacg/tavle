Who pays when the wind forecast is wrong?

Denmark sells tomorrow's electricity in an auction at noon today. A wind producer sells what it expects to make; whatever it actually makes is settled afterwards at a second price, the imbalance price. So a wind forecast error is not a statistic, it is an invoice. This page asks four questions about that invoice, states what should happen and why before looking, and then looks: what an hour of lead time is worth to a producer (act one), what the 2021 change in settlement rules did to the bill (act two), whether the system's direction is predictable and by what (act three), and whether the market knows more than the forecast (act four). Headline numbers, computed here: in DK1 a producer nominating the evening before pays in imbalance cost, and nominating an hour ahead ; the balancing direction persists from one hour to the next of the time; and adding what the wind actually did to a model of the auction price improves it by at most of R squared.

Before the study · five things to know

Denmark has two price zones, DK1 (Jutland and Funen, most of the turbines) and DK2 (Zealand and Copenhagen). Each gets its own price for every hour, set in the day-ahead auction, which closes at noon the day before delivery. Prices are in euros per megawatt-hour; a megawatt-hour runs about three hundred homes for an hour.

A wind producer sells its forecast into that auction. Energinet, the grid operator, publishes its own wind forecast for every hour at four horizons: the evening before, the same morning, five hours ahead, one hour ahead. The later the forecast, the smaller its error.

What the producer actually delivers differs from what it sold. The difference is its imbalance, and it is settled at the imbalance price: if the whole system was short of power, that price is above the auction price; if the system had too much, below. The gap on this page is the imbalance price minus the auction price. When nothing had to be regulated, the gap is zero and an imbalance costs nothing.

Since 1 November 2021 the Nordic countries use single pricing: every imbalance, helpful or harmful, settles at the same imbalance price. Before that, a producer whose error made the system's problem worse paid the regulating price and one whose error helped got only the auction price.

Everything here was written down before it was computed: the questions, what should happen and why, the baselines, the split (training to December 2023, a separate 2024 to March 2025 period), and the costs. One exploratory pass over all the data was run first to check the questions were alive; the method section says exactly what was seen.

Act one · What lead time is worth

Every hour of lead time lowers a producer's imbalance bill, and by how much is a number

hypothesisA producer that nominates a later forecast has a smaller error and so a smaller imbalance volume; its cost per megawatt-hour produced should fall with every horizon, in both zones. Baseline: the evening-before nomination, which is what a producer with no intraday desk does. Caveat registered in advance: re-nominating means trading the difference intraday at prices this data does not have, so the avoided imbalance cost is the upper bound of what the later forecast is worth.

Take a producer that owns a fixed share of its zone's wind. Nominate with each of Energinet's four forecasts in turn, deliver what the zone actually produced, settle the difference at the imbalance price, and divide the bill by the megawatt-hours produced. Act two · The rule change

Since November 2021 a third of hours cost nothing and the rest cost more

hypothesisUnder single pricing an hour with no regulation settles at the auction price, so a large share of hours should show a gap of exactly zero, where before the switch there were none; and the cost of an error that makes the system's problem worse should rise. The conditional signs should not change: more wind than forecast pushes the gap negative, less pushes it positive, in both regimes. Caveat registered in advance: the published imbalance price series is used in both eras; in the dual era a producer's wrong-direction imbalance was settled at the regulating price instead, so the dual-era bars describe the published series, not a producer's bill.

Act three · Is the direction predictable

The system stays short or long for hours, and last hour's direction beats the wind error as a signal

hypothesisThe causes of a system imbalance, weather and plant outages, last for hours, so the sign of this hour's gap should predict next hour's far better than chance and far better than the sign of the wind forecast error. Baselines: always guessing the period's majority sign; the wind-error rule. Predicted: persistence above 0.8 at one hour, falling with the lag. Caveats registered in advance: the settled gap for the previous hour is not known when the hour begins (the live signal is the activation direction Energinet publishes); deliberately creating imbalances is prohibited, so the legitimate use is an intraday position, priced with act one's caveat; and the intraday gate closes an hour before delivery, so the two-hour lag is the one a position could act on.

Pick a zone and a period. The chart is how often the sign of the gap repeats after one, two, three and six hours, against the two baselines. The table underneath costs the rule.

The costed rule: one unit long when the previous settled hour's gap was positive, short when negative, P&L per hour = predicted sign times the gap, minus 0.6 EUR/MWh. The one-hour lag is the registered rule; the two-hour lag was added the same day, labelled, because the intraday gate closes an hour before delivery. Both assume settlement at the imbalance price, which an intraday trade only approaches; read the means as upper bounds.

Act four · What the market knows

The auction prices Energinet's wind, not the wind, and the forecast has been missing low since 2023

hypothesisIf the auction had better information than Energinet's later forecast, the price would track the actual wind beyond the forecast. Add the actual-minus-forecast share to a price model that already has the forecast; the predicted change in R squared is below 0.01 in every year. Companion predictions: the forecast's bias rises in DK1 from 2023 with the steps in Energinet's installed-capacity table, and negative-price hours move from windy nights to sunny middays as solar grows.

The price model is the one from the first version of this study: hour-of-day and month effects plus expected wind as a share of consumption; its model card is in the method section. Pick a zone in each card.

Method, model card, limits, terms

How this was done, what was seen first, and what it cannot say

Data: Energinet's Forecasts_Hour at four horizons since November 2019, its hourly imbalance settlement to 4 March 2025, its settled production and consumption, its installed capacity per municipality, and the day-ahead prices carried by the tavle platform across the October 2025 dataset switch; 57 dbt tests pass on the models. Split: training December 2019 to December 2023, a separate period January 2024 to March 2025. What was seen first: the first version of this study (29 August) registered a spread strategy on the day-ahead forecast and found it untestable, because that forecast is published six hours after the auction; it is kept as written. On 30 August, before v2 was registered, one exploratory pass over all the data checked that the four hypotheses were alive; the numbers seen are listed in the pre-registration, and the 2024 to 2025 period is therefore called separate, never unread. The hypotheses, baselines, costs and definitions were then fixed and the results above computed once.

The model card for the price model used in act four

Least squares on the hourly day-ahead price with hour-of-day and month effects, with and without expected wind as a share of consumption; fitted on odd weeks and scored on even weeks of each year; error in euros per megawatt-hour against two things anyone could do without a model.

how to readLower is better. With wind the model beats yesterday's price in of zone-years, by a few euros, and loses in the volatile years: an explanation of the price, not a forecasting tool, which is all act four needs from it.

Limits

No intraday prices in the public data, so every euro figure that involves acting on information is an upper bound settled at the imbalance price. The producer in act one is a fixed share of the zone, so its errors are the zone's errors; a real portfolio's errors are larger and less correlated with the system. The dual-era bars in act two describe the published price series, not a producer's bill under the old rules. Act three's live signal is Energinet's activation data, which this page does not load. The capacity story in act four is consistent with the bias, not a proof of it. Two zones, one country, one author who chose the questions.

Six terms

day-ahead price the price fixed in the auction held the day before, one per hour per zone
imbalance price the price at which a producer's shortfall or surplus against what it sold is settled
gap imbalance price minus day-ahead price; zero when no regulation was needed
single pricing the rule since 1 November 2021 that all imbalances settle at one price
nomination the quantity a producer sells in the auction, here one of Energinet's forecasts
persistence how often the sign of the gap repeats k hours later
