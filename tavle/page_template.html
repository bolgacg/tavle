A work sample for a data-engineering role · Danish day-ahead power, public sources only

Can the desk trust the number on the screen?

Denmark buys and sells its electricity in an auction held a day in advance, and when the wind blows hard the price can fall below zero. In the last twelve months that happened in 241 hours in the western half of the country. Ask the same question of a pipeline that missed a change in the underlying dataset and it answers 894, with no error anywhere. This page is a small data platform that gets it right, the tests that keep it right, and a console where you can put your own question to the warehouse and watch it answer.

Section 1 · What you need to know first

Five sentences about Danish electricity, then the engineering

This is a work sample for a data-engineering role: a small version of the data platform a trading desk runs on, built end to end on public data so that every part of it can be shown rather than described. The subject happens to be electricity, so here is everything you need in order to read the rest. No prior knowledge is assumed anywhere on this page.

The market, in five sentences

1. Electricity is sold a day in advance.Every afternoon an auction fixes a price for each hour of tomorrow. That auction result is called the day-ahead price, and it is the number this whole page is about.
2. Denmark is split into two price zones. DK1 is everything west of the Great Belt: Jutland and Funen, where most of the wind turbines are. DK2 is Zealand and Copenhagen in the east. They are joined by one undersea link, so when that link is full the two zones can clear at different prices, and the difference between them is called the spread.
3. The unit is euros per megawatt-hour.A megawatt-hour is roughly what three hundred Danish homes use in an hour. A typical price is between 50 and 150 euros; in the 2022 energy crisis it passed 800.
4. The price can go below zero.When wind generation is high and demand is low, producers pay to keep generating rather than shut down. Negative hours are not an error in the data; counting them correctly is a real question, and it is the question this page uses to show what goes wrong.
5. All of it is published.Energinet, the Danish grid operator, publishes prices and the generation mix openly, and the European Central Bank publishes daily exchange rates. Everything here comes from those two sources and nothing else.

Section 2 · The problem

A dataset changed shape, and nothing said so

Energinet publishes the day-ahead price in two different datasets. The old one, Elspotprices, gave one price per hour and stopped on 30 September 2025. The new one, DayAheadPrices, gives one price per fifteen minutes and starts the next day. The join between them is what this page calls the seam. Both datasets have a timestamp, a zone and a price, both are correct on their own, and nothing in either one announces the change. Read them together without noticing and every hour after the switch counts four times instead of once.

Rows returned per day, across the switch

The same question, the same raw files, two pipelines. Toggle between them.

Three questions a desk would ask, each answered twice from the same raw files: once by a pipeline that reads both datasets as one pile of rows, once by this platform.

The cost. A count of negative-price hours, the sort of question a desk asks before breakfast, comes back 271 percent too high from the naive pipeline, and nothing anywhere reports an error. An average across the switch is off by four percent, which is the more dangerous number because it looks plausible.
The remedy, and what it cost. Raw stays faithful to each source. The prices mart unions the two at the seam and power_hourly averages quarter-hours to the hour, so everything downstream sees one grid. Two tests hold it: one asserts the last hourly price and the first quarter-hourly one are exactly an hour apart, the other that every hour after the switch carries four quarter-hours, which is also how a mishandled daylight-saving night announces itself. One union with an explicit boundary, two tests, both under a second.

Section 5 · The natural-language layer

A model writing the SQL, marked honestly

The menus above are deterministic; a model is not. So it is boxed in (read-only, schema-limited, row-capped, timed out) and scored on a fixed set of questions with reference answers, including three it is supposed to refuse. The scoring separates an answer that is wrong from one that is right and returns an extra column, because the first version of the scorer did not, and that made the tool look considerably worse than it was.

Read this as a floor, not a score. The questions are mine, so the number says what the guardrails catch on questions I thought of. The two remaining misses are listed above with what they were, including the one where the model's weighting is arguably better than my reference query.

Provenance

Where every number comes from

Day-ahead prices, DK1 and DK2
Energinet, Elspotprices via Energi Data Service. Hourly, January 2013 to 30 September 2025. The retired dataset.
Energinet, DayAheadPrices. Quarter-hourly, 1 October 2025 onward. Its successor, and the source of the break in section 2.
Generation and consumption
Energinet, ProductionConsumptionSettlement. Hourly settlement of wind, solar, thermal and gross consumption per area; thirty-one columns reduced to the handful a desk reads.
Reference rates
European Central Bank daily reference rates, EUR to DKK and USD. Business days only, which is the calendar mismatch the FX test exists to protect.
Engine and tooling
DuckDB as the warehouse, dbt for models and tests, Parquet for the landing zone, GitHub Actions for CI, and DuckDB compiled to WebAssembly for the console on this page.

Limits, stated rather than discovered later

Two price areas, two currencies, four datasets: this has the shape of a platform and not the scale of one. Day-ahead auction results only, because that is what Energinet publishes openly, so there is no intraday, no order book and no volume. The run ledger sits in the same DuckDB file as the marts, which is convenient here and wrong in production, where it belongs to the scheduler. The natural-language layer is scored on questions I wrote. And the whole thing is DuckDB and files: moving to Snowflake changes the adapter line in dbt and nothing in the models, which is most of the argument for writing it in dbt.
