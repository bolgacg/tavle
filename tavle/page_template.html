A work sample · data engineering for a trading desk · public data only

Can the desk trust the number on the screen?

Denmark buys its electricity in an auction held the day before, and on windy days the price goes below zero. Ask the public data how often that happened last year and a straight read says 894 hours. The true figure is 241, and nothing anywhere reports an error. This page is a small data platform that gets it right, the tests that keep it right, and a console where you can put your own question to it.

Act one · The wrong number

Hours below zero, west Denmark, last twelve months

Denmark has two price zones: DK1, the west, where most of the wind turbines stand, and DK2, the east, with Copenhagen. Below zero means producers are paying to keep generating rather than switch off, which happens when wind is high and demand is low. It is a fair question for a desk to ask before breakfast, and the answer depends entirely on how the data underneath was put together.

894
hours below zero, counting every row Energinet published as one hour
What went wrong. Energinet publishes the auction price in two datasets. The old one gave one price per hour and stopped on 30 September 2025. The new one gives one price per fifteen minutes and starts the next day. Both have a date, a zone and a price, both are correct on their own, and nothing in either says the shape changed. Read them together and every hour after the switch counts four times.
The fix, and what it cost. One table where every hour is one row on both sides of the switch, and two tests: the last hourly price and the first quarter-hourly one must be exactly an hour apart, and every hour after the switch must carry four quarter-hours. One join with a stated boundary, two tests, both under a second. The next act lets you break it and watch.

Limits

Two zones, two currencies, four datasets: the shape of a platform, not the scale. Day-ahead auction results only, because that is what is published; no intraday, no order book, no volumes. The run ledger sits in the same file as the data, which is convenient here and wrong in production. The SQL model is scored on questions I wrote. And it is DuckDB and files: moving to Snowflake changes one line in dbt and nothing in the models, which is most of the reason it is written in dbt.

Sources

Energinet, via Energi Data Service: Elspotprices (hourly, to 30 September 2025), DayAheadPrices (quarter-hourly, from 1 October 2025), ProductionConsumptionSettlement (hourly generation and consumption). European Central Bank daily reference rates. Engine: DuckDB; models and tests: dbt; landing: Parquet; CI: GitHub Actions; the console: DuckDB compiled to WebAssembly.

Five terms

day-ahead price the price fixed in the auction held the day before, one per hour per zone
EUR/MWh euros per megawatt-hour; a megawatt-hour is roughly what three hundred homes use in an hour
spread the DK1 price minus the DK2 price on the same day
wind share wind generation divided by consumption in the same hour; above 100 percent means Denmark was exporting
dbt test a query that must return no rows; the rows it returns are the failures
