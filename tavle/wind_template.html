A pre-registered study · Danish day-ahead power and Energinet's public wind forecasts

Does the wind forecast already know tomorrow's price?

Denmark prices its electricity in an auction held the day before delivery, and the biggest thing that moves that price is how hard the wind will blow. Energinet, the grid operator, publishes its own wind forecasts. So the obvious project is to predict tomorrow's price from the forecast. This page is what happens when you try that honestly: expected wind explains up to a third of the hour-to-hour price, the forecast most people would use to prove it is published six hours after the auction, and the money, if there is any, lives in what the forecast gets wrong.

Act one · The clock

Who knows what, and when

The whole study turns on a timeline. Denmark has two price zones, DK1 in the west with most of the wind and DK2 in the east with Copenhagen. For each zone, an auction closes at noon the day before delivery and sets one price per hour of the next day. Energinet publishes a wind forecast for every one of those hours, at several horizons. What this kills. The pre-registered strategy was to trade the day-ahead spread on the day-ahead wind forecast. That forecast comes out six hours after the auction that sets the price it would trade. Any model that "predicts the day-ahead price from the day-ahead wind forecast" on this dataset is looking at the answer, and it will backtest beautifully. The strategy is reported as untestable, not quietly replaced, because that finding is worth more to a desk than a green curve.
What survives. Every horizon is published before delivery, and the cost of being wrong is settled after it. So three honest questions remain: how much of the price is wind, how good the forecasts are, and what a forecast error costs. Those are the next two acts.
Act two · How much of the price is wind

Expected wind explains up to a third of the hour-to-hour price, most years a quarter

A plain model of the price with hour-of-day and month effects, fitted on odd weeks and scored on even weeks of the same year, with and without expected wind as a share of consumption. The difference is what wind adds. Pick a zone; the training years and the holdout years are shown together, and the holdout was read once, after the code was frozen.

Read this as an explanation, not a prediction. The forecast is Energinet's best public estimate of the wind the auction was pricing, published after the fact, so the gain in R squared says how much of the price is wind, not whether a desk could have known it in time. In every year and both zones the answer is a large slice, and the slice is larger in years when prices were otherwise calm.
The spread is not the wind difference. The DK1 minus DK2 price gap correlates with the difference in expected wind share at in training and on the holdout: nothing, with the sign flipping. The spread is about whether the link between the zones is full, and that is a different study.
Act three · The price of being wrong

The forecast is wrong by a few hundred megawatt-hours, and it drifts

Being wrong in this market has a price: a producer that delivers less than it sold pays the hourly imbalance price for the gap, and one that delivers more receives it. So the forecast error is not an academic number. First how large it is, then which way it costs.

The drift: monthly bias of the day-ahead forecast, actual minus forecast

A forecast that does not know about new turbines. From the winter of 2023 the day-ahead forecast under-forecasts DK1 wind by several hundred megawatt-hours in the windy months, and the gap has grown since. A bias that persists for months with the season, rather than averaging out, looks like capacity the model does not yet see. That is a finding for whoever maintains the forecast, not a weather fact, and it is why "the forecast is good" needs a date attached.

Which way being wrong costs: the imbalance gap by forecast-error decile

Right on average, wrong more often than not. When more wind arrives than the evening forecast said, the imbalance price sits below the day-ahead price on average, and above it when less arrives, in both zones and in both periods. But the sign of the gap agrees with the sign of the error in of training hours and of holdout hours, because the gap is exactly zero in of hours and small in most of the rest. The average is carried by the tails.

The added rule, scored on the holdout once

Added after the look-ahead finding and labelled as added: take the 06:00 revision of the forecast (same-morning minus day-ahead) as a signal for the sign of the imbalance gap, when the revision is large, with the pre-registered costs. A proxy for an intraday position, because intraday prices are not in the public data.

Not a strategy. A map of where one would have to live. By the pre-registered metric the rule is positive on the holdout in both zones. It is also wrong in most hours, its median hour loses exactly its costs, and its best decile of hours earns more than the whole result, meaning the other ninety percent lose money together. A desk would read that as: the information is in the large revisions on the few days the system is badly out of balance, and whether that is capturable depends on intraday prices this page does not have. I would rather show that than a curve.
What it took

How this was done, and its limits

Two more Energinet datasets added to the tavle platform: the forecasts at every horizon since November 2019, and the hourly imbalance settlement to March 2025. Fifty-seven tests pass on the models. The questions, metrics, holdout and cost model were written down before any result was computed; the changes made after reading Energinet's documentation are listed in the same file with the reason. Training is November 2019 to December 2023; the holdout from January 2024 was read exactly once.

Limits

Day-ahead auction results and hourly imbalance prices only: no intraday prices, so the added rule is a proxy and says so. Wind is offshore plus onshore as Energinet reports them, and the settlement includes small plants the forecast may not; that is one candidate for the drift, and the page does not pretend to know which. The R squared model is deliberately plain. Two zones, one country, and a study written by one person who chose the questions.

Five terms

day-ahead price the price fixed in the auction held the day before, one per hour per zone
forecast horizon how far before the hour a forecast was made: the evening before, the same morning, five hours, one hour
imbalance price what a producer pays or receives per megawatt-hour for delivering less or more than it sold
look-ahead using information in a backtest that was not available at the time of the decision
holdout the part of the data kept unread until the method was frozen, so a result cannot be tuned to it
