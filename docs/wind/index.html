Who pays when the wind forecast is wrong?

Denmark sells tomorrow's electricity in an auction at noon today. A wind producer sells what it expects to make; what it actually makes is settled afterwards at a second price. So a forecast error is an invoice. This page asks four questions about that invoice, writes down what should happen before looking, and then looks.

Before the study · five things to know

Denmark has two price zones. DK1 is Jutland and Funen, with most of the turbines. DK2 is Zealand and Copenhagen. Each gets one price per hour from the day-ahead auction, which closes at noon the day before. Prices are in euros per megawatt-hour; a megawatt-hour runs about three hundred homes for an hour.

A wind producer sells its forecast into that auction. Energinet, the grid operator, publishes a wind forecast for every hour at four horizons: the evening before, the same morning, five hours ahead, one hour ahead.

What the producer delivers differs from what it sold. That difference is its imbalance. It is settled at the imbalance price: above the auction price when the whole system was short of power, below it when the system had too much. This page calls the difference between the two prices the gap. When nothing had to be regulated the gap is zero and an imbalance costs nothing.

Since 1 November 2021 the Nordic countries use single pricing: every imbalance settles at the same imbalance price, whether it helped the system or hurt it.

Every question below was written down before it was computed, with what should happen, the baseline, the split and the costs. One exploratory pass was run first to check the questions were alive; the method section says what it saw.

Act one · What lead time is worth

A later forecast is a smaller invoice

predictionA producer that nominates a later forecast has a smaller error, so a smaller imbalance volume, so a lower cost per megawatt-hour. The cost falls with every horizon, in both zones. baselineNominating the evening-before forecast, which is what a producer without an intraday desk does. caveatRe-nominating means trading the difference intraday, at prices this data does not have. The avoided imbalance cost is the upper bound of what the later forecast is worth.

Take a producer that owns a fixed share of its zone's wind. Nominate each of Energinet's four forecasts in turn. Deliver what the zone actually produced. Settle the difference at the imbalance price. Act two · The rule change

Since November 2021, a third of hours cost nothing and the rest cost more

predictionAfter 1 November 2021 an hour with no regulation settles at the auction price, so a large share of hours should show a gap of exactly zero, where before there were none, and the remaining hours should cost more. The signs should not change: more wind than forecast pushes the gap negative, less pushes it positive. baselineThe same data before the switch. caveatThe published imbalance price series is used in both eras. Before the switch a producer's wrong-direction error was settled at the regulating price instead, so the dual-era bars describe the published series, not a producer's bill.

Act three · Is the direction predictable

The system stays short or long for hours

predictionThe causes of a system imbalance, weather and outages, last for hours. So the sign of this hour's gap predicts next hour's sign well above chance: above 0.8 at one hour, falling with the lag. baselinesAlways guessing the period's majority sign. And the rule the first version of this study used: the sign of the wind forecast error. caveatsThe previous hour's settled gap is not known when the hour begins; the live signal is the activation direction Energinet publishes. Deliberate imbalances are prohibited, so the use is an intraday position, and the intraday gate closes an hour before delivery: the two-hour lag is the one a position could act on.

Pick a zone and a period. The chart is how often the sign of the gap repeats after one, two, three and six hours, against both baselines. The table costs the rule.

how to readThree bars per lag: persistence, the majority sign, the wind-error rule. Only hours where both gaps are nonzero count. take from it

The costed rule: one unit long when the previous settled hour's gap was positive, short when negative; P&L per hour is the predicted sign times the gap, minus 0.6 EUR/MWh. The one-hour lag is the registered rule. The two-hour lag was added the same day, labelled, for the intraday gate. Both settle at the imbalance price, which an intraday trade only approaches; read the means as upper bounds.

Act four · What the market knows

The auction prices Energinet's wind, not the wind

predictionIf the auction knew more than Energinet's later forecast, the price would track the actual wind beyond the forecast. It does not: adding the actual-minus-forecast share to a price model that already has the forecast gains under 0.01 of R squared in every year. companionsThe forecast's bias in DK1 rises from 2023 with the steps in Energinet's installed-capacity table. Negative-price hours move from windy nights to sunny middays as solar grows.

Model card: the price model used in act four

Least squares on the hourly day-ahead price with hour-of-day and month effects, with and without expected wind as a share of consumption. Fitted on odd weeks, scored on even weeks of each year. Error in euros per megawatt-hour, next to two things anyone could do without a model.

how to readLower is better. With wind, the model beats yesterday's price in of zone-years, by a few euros, and loses in the volatile years. It is an explanation of the price, not a forecasting tool, which is all act four asks of it.

Limits

No intraday prices are public, so every euro figure that involves acting on information is an upper bound settled at the imbalance price. The producer in act one is a fixed share of the zone, so its errors are the zone's; a real portfolio's errors are larger. The dual-era bars in act two describe the published price series, not a producer's bill under the old rules. Act three's live signal is Energinet's activation data, which this page does not load. The capacity story in act four is consistent with the bias, not a proof of it. Two zones, one country, one author who chose the questions.

Six terms

day-ahead price the price fixed in the auction held the day before, one per hour per zone
imbalance price the price at which a producer's shortfall or surplus against what it sold is settled
gap imbalance price minus day-ahead price; zero when no regulation was needed
single pricing the rule since 1 November 2021 that all imbalances settle at one price
nomination the quantity a producer sells in the auction, here one of Energinet's forecasts
persistence how often the sign of the gap repeats k hours later
