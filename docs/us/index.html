Study · New York power market · rules fixed 6 October, single held-out run 10 and 11 October 2026

Can a model trade New York's day-ahead to real-time gap after costs?

New York's grid sells each hour of power twice: a day-ahead price is set the day before, and a real-time price as the hour happens. I built 71 strategies that bet on the gap between the two with virtual positions, paper trades settled on that gap with no power delivered, using only what was public at the 05:00 bidding deadline and paying every fee. I fixed the rules and the bar in public, built on 2013 to 2023, froze the code, and then ran every strategy once on January 2024 to September 2026, months no model had seen.

What worked on the unseen months?

28 of the 32 strategies I sent to the test cleared the profit bar I set in advance. The steadiest was a rule with no model: buy power in New York City and sell it in the Capital zone upstate, hour by hour. It made money in every year from 2013, with a Sharpe ratio (profit per unit of day-to-day swing) of 1.9 before the test and 2.3 in it.

What did the pair position and a weather model earn together?

Their daily profits are uncorrelated, so together they can run larger for the same worst loss. Held to a worst fall of 7 percent of 500,000 USD, the combined book returned 14.9 percent a year on the unseen months, 8.8 without the 18 storm and heat-wave days. The S&P 500 returned more, 18.8 percent, but with a much bumpier ride: its Sharpe ratio was 0.9 against the book's 2.2.

What did not work out as I expected?

Two weather events in 2026 carried much of the models' profit, and without them no model did better than a rule that always sells. All four verdicts I registered before the run read "inconclusive".

The bet

A virtual position bets on the gap between two prices for the same hour

Every strategy on this page is a rule for when to take this one kind of position, on which side and in which zone.

  1. NYISO, the company that runs New York's grid, sells power for each hour twice: a day-ahead price is set the day before, and a real-time price is set as the hour happens. Prices are in US dollars per megawatt-hour (MWh), about what 800 average American homes use in an hour.
  2. Virtual supply means selling power day-ahead and buying it back in real time: a bet that wins when real time comes in lower (a short position). Virtual load is the reverse, a bet that real time comes in higher (a long position). No power moves. Always supply, the plain rule on this page, sells 1 MW in every zone and every hour.
  3. A worked example: if New York City's 17:00 hour clears day-ahead at 50 USD and real-time at 40, virtual supply earns 10 USD per MWh, less fees of about 0.15 to 0.21 USD per MWh; had real time spiked to 300, it would lose 250.
  4. A zone pair buys in one of NYISO's 11 price zones and sells in another for the same hour, so a move in the overall price cancels and only the difference between the zones, mostly the cost of crowded power lines (congestion), is left.
  5. Bids, the orders for these positions, close at 05:00 New York time the day before delivery, so every input a model reads must be public by then. Fees came to about 0.15 USD per MWh in 2024, more than the 0.06 that all virtual traders in New York earned per MWh that year before fees, so losing is the expected result.
  6. The bar was fixed before any model ran. Each strategy is sized so its worst fall from a high on the build years is 100,000 USD. At that size it must earn at least 10 percent a year on a 500,000 USD bankroll, make money in most years, have a Sharpe ratio above the S&P 500's long-run 0.42, and still make money at a cost of 0.50 USD per MWh. A strategy that passed this on the build years and was not flagged fragile (leaning on a few days or a lucky setting) went to the held-out test as a candidate.

Hypothesis, not testedThe main power lines into New York City ran fuller in real time than the day-ahead market expected. A likely reason is that older plants in the city that run only at peak hours closed in May 2025 under a state emissions rule, before a new cable into the city (Champlain Hudson) began delivering. Why the premium is still there to collect is untested too; fees, collateral and the few traders active in these pairs are candidates. This page does not test either.

The combined book

Adding one weather and border model to the pair position lifts the return for the same drawdown limit, because the two are uncorrelated

The book puts 80 percent of the risk in the pair position and 20 percent in the weather and border model (V15c). The two lose money on different days, so together they can run larger for the same worst loss. Every book stayed inside its limit on the unseen months: a worst fall of 35,000 USD (7 percent of the 500,000 USD bankroll, no borrowing) or 75,000 USD (15 percent, with borrowing, where the book returned 31.8 percent a year).

How the sizes were set: I sized each book on 2013 to 2023 so its worst fall there hit the limit, then cut it to 70 percent as a margin. I chose the limits, the margin and the mix on 11 October, after the run, so the book's numbers on the unseen months describe it rather than test it.

What did not work out as I expected

Two weather events carried much of the models' profit, and no model beat its rule with no model

I expected the models to earn their profit by timing, on ordinary days. Three outcomes say they did not:

  • Two 2026 weather events, a winter storm from 24 January to 2 February and a heat wave from 28 June to 5 July, paid anyone who sold power day-ahead. Without those 18 days, the models that mostly sell did no better than always supply, the rule that sells everywhere with no model. I drew both windows after the run. The June 2025 heat wave, which cost sellers money, stays in both views. Five days of the 2026 heat wave use prices I rebuilt from NYISO's 5-minute data; their energy part matches NYISO's official generator prices within a cent.
  • All four verdicts I registered before the run read "inconclusive", so model skill is not proven.
  • On the unseen months none of the pair models beat the zone pairs with no model, and two did measurably worse.

How the test was kept clean

I fixed the rules and froze the code before the test, and every stop in the run is dated in public

A held-out result only means something if nothing was chosen after seeing it, so this section shows what was fixed when, what the run tripped over, and how each fix was kept from touching the result.

In short: I fixed the rules on 6 and 7 October and froze the code on 8 October. The freeze moved once, on 10 October, to fix a data check, and no result before 2024 changed. The run stopped four times, and each fix was made in public without changing a rule, a model or an earlier number. On the event days the models picked the winning side no more often than always supply did, where a leak would show up as a far higher hit rate. Three things came after the run and are labelled as such wherever they appear: the two event windows I leave out, singling out the pair rule, and the book (its limits, margin and mix).

I used Claude, an AI assistant, to help set up the experiments, write code and run the audits; every rule, decision and approval here is mine.

  • The objectives were committed on 6 October (7f98a86) and 7 October for version 2 (9e0416b), before any model ran; every later change is a dated addendum, never an edit.
  • The rule that picks the candidates was fixed on 7 October (99234c2), and the two lists it produced were published on 8 October (6f4c85e), with the build-year code and a fingerprint of every file (aeb7927).
  • The code was frozen on 8 October (b56c5e8). Every data loader refuses dates from 2024 unless the code is unchanged since the frozen commit and an explicit switch is set.
  • Before the test, the frozen code re-ran 2023 and had to reproduce the build-year positions. Version 1 matched exactly; version 2 matched 33 rows exactly, three deep rows within a tolerance I set (at most 4 of 96,360 zone-hours, under 50 USD over the year), and one row could not be reproduced and was dropped.

    The run log: every stop and every fix

    The run script stops at the first failed check and writes down why. It stopped four times, and two steps crashed after scoring finished; each fix below was decided and published before the run went on, and none changed a rule or a model.

    1. 8 Oct 2026Stopped at the version 2 dry run. Three deep rows differed from their build run in a handful of zone-hours, because the graphics card does not repeat its arithmetic bit for bit, and the learned mix of version 2 strategies (V12) could not reproduce its build run at all. On 9 October I set a tolerance for the first and dropped V12 (6199e84).
    2. 9 Oct 2026Stopped at the data rebuild. 300 weather forecast values for 30 and 31 December 2023 were blank in the build and filled in the rebuild, because the build had not read forecast hours that fall in 2024. They were issued before the deadline, so this is missing data, not a leak; I accepted the rebuilt table (c174584).
    3. 10 Oct 2026Stopped at version 1 scoring. NYISO's hourly real-time archive lacks 1 to 18 and 20 July 2026, and my input check had missed it because it counted monthly files, not the days inside them. A first fix that skipped those days (0d6185f) was reverted before any run used it (e3118db). I rebuilt the days from NYISO's own 5-minute prices, fixed the check to count days (f0636cc) and moved the freeze to that commit (5856c45); every row before 2024 stayed identical.
    4. 10 Oct 2026Stopped at the data rebuild again on the same 300 weather values, and went on under the rule of 9 October.
    5. 11 Oct 2026Scoring finished. The two steps after it crashed: the summary, because always supply's placebo field holds a sentence instead of numbers, and the chart data, because the daily table keeps three rows that never traded. Both were rebuilt outside the frozen code from the scored files; the chart data script is page code and its fix is in this page's commit.     What the audit of the results checked

    • Every audited row's daily profit was recomputed from its positions and NYISO's prices and matched the scorers to 0.00 USD on every day; the version 1 and version 2 price tables agree on all 265,045 zone-hours of the window.
    • No real-time price is stamped before its hour ends, and no price of a delivery day is stamped before 05:00 on the bid day; the one input that matches a bid-day price in 3.8 percent of rows is the last hour already published, which the registration allows.
    • On the event days the models picked the winning side in 54 to 69 percent of the zone-hours they traded, against 68.4 percent for always supply. A model that could see real time would be right far more often; these mostly sold, as always supply did.
    • The rebuilt July days match NYISO's own generator energy prices within 0.01 USD in every hour. They were unusual days, real time 7.55 USD below day-ahead on average, and setting them aside shrinks July a lot but changes no verdict. After rounding to the cent, 3,930 of 4,320 official July zone-hours match the rebuild exactly and the rest differ by 1 cent (addendum of 11 October).
    • Two fee rates rest on the tariff and planning values, not on NYISO postings: 2025 (the tariff's cap) and 2026 (unconfirmed), 21 of the 33 months; the stress runs at 0.50 USD per MWh cover them.

    The audit, the four stop reports' outcomes and every table behind this page are in docs/us/results: AUDIT.md, pre_vs_heldout.md, the scorers' tables v2_results_heldout.md and lab_results_heldout.md, the registered verdict run registered_verdicts_heldout.json and the summary rebuilt outside the frozen code, DONE_audit.md.

    Where this study is weakest

    • Two weather events decide most totals on the unseen months, as one storm, Elliott in December 2022, did on the build years; 33 months hold too few such days to separate skill from luck for a strategy that sells. I defined the two windows after the run, and both paid the sellers; a June 2025 heat wave that cost always supply 113,888 USD in a month stays in both views.
    • Bids are assumed to clear at the market price without moving it, which flatters storm days most.
    • I tried 71 strategy rows and hundreds of settings on the build years; the held-out test was the check on that, and it found no model skill beyond the rules with no model.
    • Ideas 1 and 2 came from exploring the build years, and a reviewer saw 2024 summary statistics before the freeze; both are disclosed in the objectives.

    Terms

    Terms used on this page

    virtual supply selling power day-ahead and buying it back in real time: a short position, betting the price falls (traders say INC)
    virtual load buying day-ahead and selling in real time: a long position, betting the price rises (a DEC)
    load zone one of NYISO's 11 pricing regions, A (West) to K (Long Island)
    build years the years models were tuned and scored on: 2021 to 2023, or 2013 to 2023 in version 2
    held-out run the single run on January 2024 to September 2026, the unseen months, from the frozen code
    zone pair buy in one price zone and sell in another for the same hour, so only the difference between the zones counts
    congestion the part of a price caused by full power lines; it makes prices differ between zones
    N.Y.C., CAPITL, WEST NYISO's New York City zone (J), Capital zone around Albany (F) and West zone around Buffalo (A)
    sell-side model a model whose positions are mostly virtual supply, so it wins when real time comes in below day-ahead
    event days the 18 days of the winter storm (24 January to 2 February 2026) and the heat wave (28 June to 5 July 2026)
    correlation how closely two daily profit series move together, from minus 1 to plus 1; near 0 means unrelated
    drawdown limit the largest fall from a high I accept: 7 percent of 500,000 USD without leverage, 15 percent with it
    95 percent interval the range that 95 of 100 resampled histories fall in; one that crosses zero shows no difference
    walk-forward each month or quarter scored by a model trained only on earlier data
    sizing view the size at which a strategy's worst build-year drop is 100,000 USD, at most 5 MW
    Sharpe ratio average daily profit divided by its day-to-day swings, scaled to a year; the S&P 500's long-run value is about 0.42
    fragile negative without its best ten days, flips at a nearby setting, beaten by random days, or failed an audit
    habit rule no model: every zone and hour takes the side that won on average over the past year
    drawdown the fall from a running high to the next low
    NYISO the New York Independent System Operator, which runs the state's grid and its power markets

    Audits

    What the audits caught

    Reviews and audits found four problems before the held-out run. Each is listed with what it changed, so a reader can judge how much to trust the numbers above.

    1. The ideas were chosen after the building years were seen. The storm-day filter and the storm-day flip were found in an exploration of 2020 to 2023 prices, Winter Storm Elliott included, so 2022 is not out of sample for their design. Before the freeze, a reviewer also looked at descriptive statistics for all years, 2024 onward included. These statistics motivated idea A, and no model has seen 2024 onward. Both facts are written in the objectives file as disclosures.
    2. Two side scripts loaded the whole price table, including 2024 onward, and dropped those rows in the next statement. No value from 2024 onward was used, computed, printed or saved. A separate review pass found it, and both scripts now filter at read time. The locked loaders already worked that way.
    3. Clock times in the objectives file were wrong twice. The ideas were registered at 20:48 and 21:07 Danish time, not the times first written. A disclosure was committed at 21:18, not 21:30. The commit times on GitHub are the record, and later addenda carry a date only. One timing bug in a storm script also set the 05:00 deadline as five elapsed hours, which is 06:00 on the day clocks go forward. It let in 33 price rows and 22 weather rows and changed no decision.
    4. The storm rules are fragile. Two Elliott days, 23 and 24 December 2022, give the storm-day flip 204,836 USD of its 2022 profit. Outside them its virtual load trades earned 8,515 over three years. The storm-day filter's 2022 result exists because those two days scored 0.904 and 0.901 against a cut of 0.90. Idea 10, the weather surprise, earns 204,836 of its 352,130 in the same two days. All three were moved to the fragile group, and the audits reproduced every figure to the dollar.

    The arithmetic held and the evidence did not. Every audited number was reproduced and no lookahead changed a result. The storm strategies still rest on one event, and the audits say so in the files storm_audit.md and idea10_audit.md.

    Weaknesses

    Where this study is weakest

    A reader should know the limits before repeating any number from this page.

    • Too few storm days, and too little training data. The building years hold one storm of the size that drives the biggest profits, and a model cannot learn a pattern from one example. Version 2 trains on 2010 to 2023 to give it more, and it is the planned answer to this weakness.
    • Decay in 2023. B with gradient boosting lost 22,361 USD in 2023 and B deep lost 35,731, against gains in 2022. The market monitor reports the profit of all virtual traders fell from about 20 million USD in 2022 to about 1.2 million in 2023, so the pool itself shrank. The two numbers use different methods and are not directly comparable.
    • B leans on 2022. B with gradient boosting earned 555,120 of its 583,946 in 2022, and 180,520 of that came from the two Elliott days. Its inputs pass an independent rebuild at 05:00, so the 2022 figure is real data. It is still one year.
    • Bids are assumed to fill. Every position is assumed to clear at the market price, with no effect of its own on prices. A storm-day load bid must be priced near the cap to clear, so the assumption is most generous on the days that matter most.
    • Public data only. Everything used was public by 05:00 New York time the day before. A trader with the grid operator's internal data, or a faster weather feed, would do differently.
    • One market. The study covers New York only. Whether any result carries to another grid is untested.

    Most likely failure on the held-out years, as written before the run. No storm of Elliott's size lands, the B models earn their 2023 pattern, and the candidates trail always taking supply in calm months. What happened. A storm did land, in January 2026, but real time ran below day-ahead, so it paid the sellers; the B models earned most of their unseen-month profit in it, and without the storm and the June heat wave they were level with always supply.

    Glossary

    Terms in plain words

    Each term below is used in the results, the audits or the weaknesses.

    DART spread the difference between the day-ahead and real-time price for one zone and one hour. This page writes it as real-time minus day-ahead, and papers differ on the sign
    INCs and DECs the trader's names for virtual positions. An INC (increment) sells day-ahead power and buys it back in real time, which is virtual supply. A DEC (decrement) does the opposite, which is virtual load
    virtual bid an offer to buy or sell power day-ahead with no power delivered. It is settled by the difference between the day-ahead and real-time price, and the trader pays a fee per cleared MWh
    FTR or TCC a financial right that pays the congestion price between two points. In New York it is called a TCC, in other grids an FTR. Some traders move day-ahead prices to raise its value
    LBMP location-based marginal price, the price of one more MWh at one place, as NYISO names its zone and bus prices
    load zone one of NYISO's 11 pricing regions, lettered A (West) to K (Long Island). Zone prices are what virtual bids settle against
    congestion the part of a price caused by a transmission line being full. It makes prices differ between places, and it is what idea C and idea D trade
    Sharpe ratio average daily profit divided by the daily spread of profit, scaled to a year. A higher number means steadier profit for the same average
    drawdown the fall from a running profit peak to the next low. The maximum drawdown is the worst such fall in the period
    walk-forward scoring each month using a model trained only on earlier data, then moving one month ahead and repeating, so no month predicts itself
    held-out data kept unread while building. The held-out years, January 2024 to September 2026, are used once, after the code is frozen
    sizing view the position size that would make the worst fall from a peak 100,000 USD over 2021 to 2023, at most 5 MW
    fragile a flag for a strategy that turns negative without its best ten days, changes sign at a neighbouring setting, or is beaten by random days in the same months
    Related work

    What others found about virtual trading in New York and elsewhere

    These papers and reports are the closest comparison points, and several of them report what this study found: the profit pool is thin and a few stress events carry it.

    1. Potomac Economics, NYISO State of the Market 2024 and the 2022 report: net virtual profit of about 1.8 million USD in 2024, 0.06 USD per MWh, against about 20 million gross in 2022. The reports name Long Island as the zone most prone to real-time premiums. The two years use different profit methods.
    2. Hubert, Lolas and Sircar, Trading Electrons: Predicting DART Spread Spikes in ISO Electricity Markets (2026) is the closest match. It trades NYISO's 11 zones, trains on 2015 to 2019 and tests on 2022 to 2025, and earns about 6 million USD, nearly all in Long Island, with sell trades ahead of buy trades. It models no transaction costs.
    3. Galarneau-Vincent, Gauthier and Godin, Foreseeing the worst: forecasting electricity DART spikes (Energy Economics, 2023) predicts the probability of a spike in the Long Island zone and trades on it. Only the abstract was available to me.
    4. Baltaoglu, Tong and Zhao, Algorithmic Bidding for Virtual Trading in Electricity Markets tests an online learner on NYISO and PJM data from 2011 to 2016. It beat the S&P 500 on Sharpe ratio, and its profits fell over the years as prices converged. It assumes the trader does not move prices.
    5. Birge, Hortacsu, Mercadal and Pavlin, Limits to Arbitrage in Electricity Markets (MISO, 2018) finds that some large traders bought day-ahead against the premium and lost for years, apparently to raise the value of their congestion rights. A losing pattern is therefore not always noise.
    6. Parsons and co-authors, Financial Arbitrage and Efficient Dispatch (MIT CEEPR, 2015) argues that day-ahead and real-time prices differ because the two market programs make different approximations. Profit for a virtual trader is therefore no evidence that the trader improves the market, and this page does not claim it.
    7. Capponi, Iyengar, Yang and Bienstock, Virtual Trading in Multi-Settlement Electricity Markets (2025) is a theory paper. With enough virtual traders the price gap closes, which fits the shrinking edge seen here.

    1 · The bet

    What a virtual position is, and what it costs

    Every idea on this page is a rule for when to take one kind of position and on which side, so the position is defined here once.

    NYISO, the operator of New York's power grid, prices electricity twice. The day-ahead market sets a price for every hour of tomorrow, and the real-time market prices the same hour again as it happens, from what the grid actually needed. Prices are in US dollars per megawatt-hour (USD per MWh); one megawatt-hour is roughly what 800 average American homes use in an hour.

    A virtual position trades the difference between those two prices without producing or using any power. Virtual supply sells 1 MW in the day-ahead market and buys it back at the real-time price, so in each hour it earns the day-ahead price minus the real-time price. Virtual load does the opposite. NYISO takes virtual bids only at its 11 load zones, the regions it prices from West, around Buffalo, to Long Island.

    Bids for a delivery day close at 05:00 New York time the day before. Day-ahead results usually appear near 09:50, so the position is fixed about five hours before anyone sees the day-ahead price it will get. Section 4 checks every input against that deadline.

    NYISO charges a fee on every cleared virtual megawatt-hour. It was 0.1066 USD in 2023 and 0.1333 in 2024. In 2026 it is 0.1919, 80 percent more than in 2023.

    That fee is a high bar here. NYISO's market monitor reports that all virtual trading in New York earned 0.06 USD per MWh on average in 2024, before fees, which is less than that year's fee. A result in which no idea pays is therefore the expected outcome, and it will be reported as one.

    Terms used below

    day-ahead price the price set the day before for each hour of tomorrow, one per zone
    real-time price the price set as the hour happens; this study uses the hourly value
    gap real-time price minus day-ahead price, for one zone and one hour
    virtual supply, virtual load selling or buying 1 MW day-ahead and reversing it at the real-time price
    load zone one of NYISO's 11 pricing regions, lettered A (West) to K (Long Island)
    extreme 1% of hours in each zone, the 1% of hours with the largest gap in either direction
    baseline the rule every idea must beat: in each zone and hour, take the side the gap favoured on average over the past 365 days
    gradient boosting a standard machine-learning method that adds many small decision trees, each correcting the errors of the ones before
    sequence model a neural network, here written in PyTorch, that reads recent prices hour by hour
    build years, held-out run 2020 to 2023 are for building and tuning; January 2024 to September 2026 is run once, after the code is frozen
    bootstrap over whole days resampling whole days many times to put a 95% interval around daily profit
    Holm correction a rule that raises the bar when several ideas are tested, so one lucky idea out of four is not called a win
    break-even fee the fee at which an idea's profit would fall to zero
    GFS the Global Forecast System, the US weather service's public forecast model
    2 · Four ideas

    Four ideas, one of them the main test

    Testing four ideas raises the chance that one wins by luck. Idea A is therefore named the main test in advance, and the other three face a stricter bar (objective 5 below).

    Idea A · the main test

    Take the steady side, and sit out the hours a model flags as spike risk

    In the build years, real-time settled below day-ahead in most hours in ten of the 11 zones, so virtual supply usually won. It still lost money in nine of them, because of a few hours when real-time jumped as much as 2,900 USD per MWh above day-ahead (section 5). Idea A takes virtual supply by default and stays out of the hours a model flags as spike risk the day before. It pays only if the model avoids enough spikes to beat the baseline after fees.

    Idea B

    Predict where NYISO's load forecast will be wrong

    NYISO commits extra generators in the day-ahead market to cover its own forecast of tomorrow's demand, which the industry calls load. If public weather forecasts can tell when that load forecast will run high or low, real-time prices should miss day-ahead in a predictable direction. B uses archived GFS weather forecasts issued two days before each hour; these start in 2021, so B is built on 2021 to 2023.

    Idea C

    Trade the price difference between two zones

    Power flows from upstate New York toward New York City and Long Island. When the lines between them fill up, which is called congestion, the zone prices split, and that pattern repeats by hour and season. C takes virtual supply in one zone and virtual load in another, so a move in the overall price level cancels and only the change in the difference between the two zones counts.

    Idea D

    Trade congestion that planned outages make predictable

    NYISO publishes the transmission outages it has scheduled, and the latest list is public before the bidding deadline. A line taken out of service limits how much power can move between zones, which can push zone prices apart in a known direction. D tests whether the day-ahead price already allows for that, using the schedule as published, including outages that were later cancelled.

    The exact wording of all four ideas, and the rules that will score them, follow as they were fixed.

    How the charts are made

    Every chart on this page is drawn by a short script inside the page from the JSON files linked under it, which the chart data script writes from the scored results; no chart number is typed. The JSON files carry their own source, unit and a holdout line saying that nothing on or after 1 January 2024 was read.

    The build-year charts read files written with nothing after 31 December 2023.
