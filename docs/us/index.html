Study plan · New York's wholesale power market · fixed before any model ran

Can a model trade New York's day-ahead to real-time gap after costs?

This study tests four ideas for trading the gap between New York's day-ahead and real-time power prices, using only what is public at the bidding deadline and paying the grid operator's fee on every megawatt-hour. The rules, the test period and the bar for success were fixed and published on 6 October 2026, before any model ran.

What is tested?Four ideas for a 1 MW virtual position, each scored against a plain baseline on January 2024 to September 2026, in one run.
Why is it hard?New York's market monitor reports that all virtual trading there earned 0.06 USD per MWh on average in 2024 before fees, less than that year's fee of 0.1333.
What has run so far?No model. One data check on the build years 2020 to 2023, shown in section 5, is the reason for the main idea.
When is the answer due?The single held-out run is planned for 11 October 2026. Each idea then gets one word: pays, doesn't pay, inconclusive, or not run.

1 · The bet

What a virtual position is, and what it costs

Every idea on this page is a rule for when to take one kind of position and on which side, so the position is defined here once.

NYISO, the operator of New York's power grid, prices electricity twice. The day-ahead market sets a price for every hour of tomorrow, and the real-time market prices the same hour again as it happens, from what the grid actually needed. Prices are in US dollars per megawatt-hour (USD per MWh); one megawatt-hour is roughly what 800 average American homes use in an hour.

A virtual position trades the difference between those two prices without producing or using any power. Virtual supply sells 1 MW in the day-ahead market and buys it back at the real-time price, so in each hour it earns the day-ahead price minus the real-time price. Virtual load does the opposite. NYISO takes virtual bids only at its 11 load zones, the regions it prices from West, around Buffalo, to Long Island.

Bids for a delivery day close at 05:00 New York time the day before. Day-ahead results usually appear near 09:50, so the position is fixed about five hours before anyone sees the day-ahead price it will get. Section 4 checks every input against that deadline.

NYISO charges a fee on every cleared virtual megawatt-hour. It was 0.1066 USD in 2023 and 0.1333 in 2024. In 2026 it is 0.1919, 80 percent more than in 2023.

That fee is a high bar here. NYISO's market monitor reports that all virtual trading in New York earned 0.06 USD per MWh on average in 2024, before fees, which is less than that year's fee. A result in which no idea pays is therefore the expected outcome, and it will be reported as one.

Terms used below

day-ahead price the price set the day before for each hour of tomorrow, one per zone
real-time price the price set as the hour happens; this study uses the hourly value
gap real-time price minus day-ahead price, for one zone and one hour
virtual supply, virtual load selling or buying 1 MW day-ahead and reversing it at the real-time price
load zone one of NYISO's 11 pricing regions, lettered A (West) to K (Long Island)
extreme 1% of hours in each zone, the 1% of hours with the largest gap in either direction
baseline the rule every idea must beat: in each zone and hour, take the side the gap favoured on average over the past 365 days
gradient boosting a standard machine-learning method that adds many small decision trees, each correcting the errors of the ones before
sequence model a neural network, here written in PyTorch, that reads recent prices hour by hour
build years, held-out run 2020 to 2023 are for building and tuning; January 2024 to September 2026 is run once, after the code is frozen
bootstrap over whole days resampling whole days many times to put a 95% interval around daily profit
Holm correction a rule that raises the bar when several ideas are tested, so one lucky idea out of four is not called a win
break-even fee the fee at which an idea's profit would fall to zero
GFS the Global Forecast System, the US weather service's public forecast model
2 · Four ideas

Four ideas, one of them the main test

Testing four ideas raises the chance that one wins by luck. Idea A is therefore named the main test in advance, and the other three face a stricter bar (objective 5 below).

Idea A · the main test

Take the steady side, and sit out the hours a model flags as spike risk

In the build years, real-time settled below day-ahead in most hours in ten of the 11 zones, so virtual supply usually won. It still lost money in nine of them, because of a few hours when real-time jumped as much as 2,900 USD per MWh above day-ahead (section 5). Idea A takes virtual supply by default and stays out of the hours a model flags as spike risk the day before. It pays only if the model avoids enough spikes to beat the baseline after fees.

Idea B

Predict where NYISO's load forecast will be wrong

NYISO commits extra generators in the day-ahead market to cover its own forecast of tomorrow's demand, which the industry calls load. If public weather forecasts can tell when that load forecast will run high or low, real-time prices should miss day-ahead in a predictable direction. B uses archived GFS weather forecasts issued two days before each hour; these start in 2021, so B is built on 2021 to 2023.

Idea C

Trade the price difference between two zones

Power flows from upstate New York toward New York City and Long Island. When the lines between them fill up, which is called congestion, the zone prices split, and that pattern repeats by hour and season. C takes virtual supply in one zone and virtual load in another, so a move in the overall price level cancels and only the change in the difference between the two zones counts.

Idea D

Trade congestion that planned outages make predictable

NYISO publishes the transmission outages it has scheduled, and the latest list is public before the bidding deadline. A line taken out of service limits how much power can move between zones, which can push zone prices apart in a known direction. D tests whether the day-ahead price already allows for that, using the schedule as published, including outages that were later cancelled.

The exact wording of all four ideas, and the rules that will score them, follow as they were fixed.

3 · The objectives

The objectives, as fixed on 6 October 2026

Fixing the question, the test period and the bar before any model runs means the result cannot be tuned toward an answer. The text below is the approved objectives file, reproduced word for word.

Fixed on 6 October 2026, before any model ran. Never edited.Later changes: dated addenda below the line

1. Question. Using only what is public by 05:00 New York time the day before delivery, which of four ideas turns New York's day-ahead to real-time price gap into profit after costs for a 1 MW virtual position? Positions are assumed to clear at the market price.

A (main test). Take the steady downward gap, and sit out the hours a model flags as spike risk.

B. Use public weather forecasts to predict where the operator's load forecast runs wrong, and trade on it.

C. Bet up in one zone and down in another, to trade the repeating congestion patterns.

D. Trade the congestion that planned transmission outages make predictable.

2. Data. Day-ahead and real-time prices for the 11 tradable zones, January 2020 to September 2026, with generator and border points as inputs only, each used only while it existed. The operator's load forecast issued before the deadline. Archived weather forecasts for B. Outage notices for D. Every input carries its publication time; a test fails the build if anything was published after 05:00 New York time.

3. Models. Baseline: always take each zone-hour's usual side (average gap over the trailing 365 days). Gradient boosting. A PyTorch sequence model averaged over three seeds, trained on one 6 GB consumer GPU, used inside each idea where it fits. All tuning on 2020 to 2023 only, with a rehearsal on 2023. Code and settings frozen in a public commit before the held-out run. Anything not frozen by 10 Oct 23:59 is reported as not run.

4. Test. January 2024 to September 2026, run once. Each month predicted only from data up to two days earlier. Any rerun after a bug fix is published beside the original with the difference.

5. Score. Each idea against the baseline: daily net profit after each year's NYISO virtual charge, 95% interval from a bootstrap over whole days. Primary: A. Secondary in order B, C, D, with Holm correction. Ranking table: profit per MWh, break-even fee, worst month, results without the most extreme 1% of hours; costs stressed at 0.50 and 1.00 USD per MWh. Deep against gradient boosting reported inside every idea.

6. Bar (never edited). Each idea gets one word: pays, doesn't pay, inconclusive, or not run. Pays = lower end of its corrected interval above zero. Deep against gradient boosting: better, worse, equivalent (interval inside plus or minus one fee per MWh), or inconclusive.

7. Delivery. 6 Oct plan page live. 8 Oct data, timing test, data checks for B and D. 9 Oct baselines and rehearsal. 10 Oct freeze. 11 Oct single held-out run. From 13 Oct every idea that ran publishes next-day positions before 05:00 New York time, labelled a pipeline test, until an interview. 14 Oct page complete.

Addenda (dated; written before any model has run)

6 Oct 2026, data checks for B and D (done the same day, before any model):

  • B: NYISO's own load-forecast weather file (lfweather) is written about 08:00 New York time and its forecast for the delivery day first appears after the 05:00 deadline, so it is not used as a bid input. B uses archived GFS forecasts (Open-Meteo previous-runs archive), taking the run issued two days before each target hour; available from 2021, so B's building window is 2021 to 2023.
  • D: NYISO outSched, a daily snapshot of scheduled transmission outages (about 100 lines), written about 09:40 New York time the day before its date, archived from January 2020. Scheduled, not actual: some outages are cancelled.
  • Disclosure: before the freeze, a reviewer scanned descriptive statistics across all years, 2024 onward included (real-time below day-ahead in most zone-years; NYC 2024 always-supply earned 0.00 USD per MWh, 1.74 without its worst 1% of hours). These motivated idea A. No model has seen 2024 onward.

6 Oct 2026, evening, data layer built (before any model):

  • Timing test: on 500 random bid days from 2020 to 2026, no row used for delivery day D+1 was published after 05:00 New York time on day D. Lookahead rows injected on 20 of those days (the next day's load forecast file, the target day's own day-ahead price, the real-time price for the hour starting 05:00, the next day's outage snapshot, late weather values) were all caught. 30 of 30 tests pass.
  • B's weather archive has values from 25 March 2021, not 1 January 2021; B's building window starts there.
  • Weather timing: the archive's “two days before” value is the forecast made 48 hours before the valid time. To stay safe even if the nearest model run is up to three hours later and takes up to five hours to publish, B uses the two-day value only for delivery hours up to 21:00 and the three-day value for 22:00 and 23:00.
  • Real-time price corrections: NYISO rewrote 18 to 52 zone files a year after the day. A corrected day's prices count as public only from the rewrite time, so on those days the previous day's real-time prices are not used at 05:00.

6 Oct 2026, night, modelling contract (before any model ran; research/us/model/CONTRACT.md):

  • Each idea's verdict uses gradient boosting; the deep model runs inside every idea in its place for the secondary comparison.
  • Positions: A is supply only (supply when the predicted gap is at or below minus the fee, else none). B and D trade both sides (supply at or below minus the fee, load at or above plus the fee, else none). C trades up to five zone pairs chosen on the build years, when the predicted difference is at least twice the fee.
  • The holdout is locked in code: every loader refuses delivery dates from 1 January 2024 unless a freeze commit is named and the model code is unchanged since it.

6 Oct 2026, late evening, after the 2023 rehearsal (2023 is a build year; no held-out data has been read):

  • The rehearsal is published as it ran. Gradient boosting's predicted gap barely tracked the real gap in 2023 (hourly correlation 0.011, against 0.111 on 2022), its spike warnings did not come true, and idea A under the regression rule earned 112 USD a day on its own, less than always taking supply (198). The deep model had no skill either.
  • Idea A's rule is changed to match its own wording: both models now predict the probability that an hour is a spike, and A takes virtual supply in every zone-hour except those whose spike probability exceeds a cut. The spike threshold and the cut are chosen on 2020 to 2022 only, then rehearsed on 2023. B, C and D are unchanged.
  • Verdict wording made exact: “doesn't pay” means the upper end of the corrected interval is below zero. For deep against gradient boosting, better and worse are checked first; equivalent applies only when the interval contains zero and lies inside plus or minus one fee per MWh.
  • Shown beside every verdict as context, not as tests: each idea's own net profit with its interval; idea A against always taking supply; and return on a bankroll fixed before the freeze plus the Sharpe ratio, beside the S&P 500 and a Danish bank deposit rate over the same months.
  • Audit fixes before the freeze: a verdict could read “pays” when the Holm step had not rejected, now consistent; for the rehearsal, idea D's most frequent outage sites are counted only on data before 2023.

6 Oct 2026, evening, after the cost and capital audit (cost_capital_audit.md; before the freeze):

  • Fees corrected from NYISO postings: Rate Schedule 1 is charged once per cleared day-ahead virtual MWh; 2021 is 0.0757 USD and 2025 is capped at 0.1666 by the tariff's 25 percent annual limit. The base cost now also includes the FERC annual charge (about 0.01 to 0.02 per MWh) and, for virtual supply only, an upper bound for its share of uplift (0.003 to 0.03). The 0.50 and 1.00 stress costs are unchanged.
  • Bankroll for the return comparison, fixed from 2020 to 2023 only: 500,000 USD (the 200,000 capitalization deposit NYISO asks of a small entity, about 90,000 of collateral, and 195,000 for the full book's worst 12-day loss, around Winter Storm Elliott).
  • Because a return on bankroll depends on the bankroll chosen, the Sharpe ratio and the return over maximum drawdown are reported beside it, next to the S&P 500 and Danish deposit rates over the same months.

6 Oct 2026, night, a gate before the held-out run:

  • The held-out years stay unread until the build years show something worth testing. Every strategy is first scored out of sample inside 2020 to 2023 (a walk-forward over 2021 to 2023, each month predicted only from earlier data), with the corrected costs, and the full list is approved in writing before the freeze.
  • The held-out run then includes every strategy on that list, the strong, the weak and the basic ones (always taking supply, the baseline, the simple hourly-mean version of idea A), so all of them face the same unseen years. If the gate is not yet passed, the freeze and held-out dates move; the milestone table shows the actual dates.

6 Oct 2026, 20:48 Danish time (corrected), ten registered ideas in one family (written before ideas 3 to 10 were run):

Take virtual supply on calm days, and on dangerous days either sit out or take virtual load. The models' only job is to tell the days and hours apart using what is public by 05:00. The ten ideas, the bar and the safeguards are in OBJECTIVES.md: two hand-made storm rules found in exploration earlier the same day (disclosed as such), four deep-learning variants, one gradient-boosting comparison, storm-day zone pairs, an extreme-tail spike flag and a weather-surprise rule. Every idea must pass the publication-time test, an injected-lookahead test and two placebos (a score shifted one day late and a shuffled score), with every choice made only on earlier years. All of them, the basic strategies and ideas A to D then face the held-out years together.

6 Oct 2026, 21:07 Danish time (corrected), two more registered ideas (before either ran):

11, a learned allocator: a deep gate network weighs four simple strategies (supply, sit out, load, the storm-day zone pair) for each zone and day, trained only on earlier days. 12, an end-to-end policy: a deep network outputs each zone's next-day position directly and is trained on profit after costs with a penalty on losses. Ideas 3 to 6 predict and then decide by a rule; 11 learns how to combine strategies; 12 learns the decision itself. Same bar, same safeguards; both count in the number of ideas tried.

6 Oct 2026, size and the bar (before the freeze):

Profit at 1 MW is an arbitrary scale; the Sharpe ratio is not. Every strategy keeps its 1 MW verdict, and beside it a sizing view: the MW per zone-hour at which its worst 2021 to 2023 drawdown equals 100,000 USD, capped at 5 MW for collateral and price impact, with the return on the 500,000 USD bankroll at that size. The 10 percent a year bar for the held-out list is judged on that view, only for strategies not flagged fragile, and the size is carried unchanged into the held-out run.

6 Oct 2026, a registered combination and the overnight checks (before the freeze):

13, a portfolio of the survivors: every strategy that passes at the sizing view and is not flagged fragile, each scaled to the same risk budget and combined with equal risk weights fixed from the build years. Before the list is approved, the strongest rows also face robustness checks on 2021 to 2023 only: 20 random starts per deep model (the spread is reported, never the best start), the neighbouring settings, costs at 1.00 USD per MWh, and results by season and half-year. These checks can add a fragile flag; they cannot change a rule.

4 · Timing

What is known at 05:00, and what is not

A backtest that uses anything published after 05:00 makes money no trader could have made. Objective 2 therefore gives every input its publication time and makes the build fail if one is late; that test is written on 8 October.

Publication times were checked on 6 October 2026 against the timestamps of NYISO's archived files and NYISO's own documents (section 7).

Two limits. Settlement uses the corrected real-time prices, so profits are computed correctly, but as an input a corrected price for a recent hour may differ from what was on screen at 05:00 that day. Positions are also assumed to clear at the market price, which a real bid with a price limit may not.

In 2020 to 2023, real time settled below day ahead in most hours in ten of eleven zones, yet always taking the virtual-supply side lost money in nine of them, because of the most extreme 1 percent of hours. WEST is the zone where it earned money (0.55 USD per MWh); NORTH is the zone where real time was below day ahead in only 48 percent of hours. Without the most extreme 1 percent of hours, the same bet earned 1.00 to 2.29 USD per MWh in ten zones.

Those hours were picked with hindsight, so this is a premise, not a strategy. Idea A asks whether a model can flag enough of them by 05:00 the day before.
